# 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews the research behind the project design. It starts with food waste in food service and the part overproduction plays in it, then covers demand forecasting in restaurants and more widely. It then examines the Long Short Term Memory network and Prophet, the two time series methods that started the project, and the studies comparing them. Next come fair testing of forecasts, how forecasts become preparation decisions, how unusual days can be found, and what decision support research expects from a usable system. The last section draws these into the research gap and the four research questions. The review focuses on peer reviewed studies, well known textbooks and official reports. Every figure is credited to its source and is not a result of this project.

## 2.2 Food waste in food service

Food waste is a global problem. A study for the Food and Agriculture Organization estimated that about one third of food produced for people, about 1.3 billion tonnes a year, is lost or wasted along the food chain (Gustavsson et al., 2011). Newer estimates put food wasted in shops, food service and homes in 2022 at 1.05 billion tonnes, about one fifth of the food available to consumers, with around 290 million tonnes from food service (United Nations Environment Programme, 2024). The estimates differ in scope and method, but both place food service among the sectors where cutting waste matters.

National studies show where food service waste arises. For the United Kingdom, WRAP (2013) estimated that hospitality and food service outlets produce about 920,000 tonnes of food waste a year, of which 75 per cent could have been eaten. The waste comes from spoilage (21 per cent), food preparation (45 per cent) and food left on customer plates (34 per cent). It costs an estimated 2.5 billion pounds a year. Betz et al. (2015) measured waste in two Swiss catering businesses and found that 10.73 per cent and 7.69 per cent of the food mass delivered was wasted, most of it avoidable. Such measurements are rare because waste must be weighed on site, but they show that much food service waste is avoidable.

Not all of this waste is linked to forecasting. Plate waste depends on portion sizes and customer behaviour, and spoilage on storage and stock rotation, but a real share comes from preparing more food than is sold. Papargyropoulou et al. (2016) link food waste in hospitality to the ways food is provided, including deciding how much to buy and prepare before demand is known. So overproduction is the part better demand information can help most directly.

The food waste hierarchy gives this a clear priority. Papargyropoulou et al. (2014) argue that stopping extra food from being made is better than giving it away, recycling it or turning it into energy, with throwing it away the worst option. Better preparation planning works at the top of this hierarchy, but this limits what can be claimed. A forecasting system can only reduce waste from preparing too much, and its effect is only known if waste is measured.

## 2.3 Demand forecasting in restaurants

Restaurant demand mixes regular patterns and irregular events. Lasek, Cercone and Saunders (2016) reviewed research on restaurant sales and demand forecasting, grouping methods from simple statistics to machine learning, and noted that the right method depends on the data and the purpose of the forecast. Tanizaki et al. (2019) argued that a useful forecast in a real store must include factors special to that store, such as location, weather and local events, and built machine learning models joining these inputs with point of sale records. Their work shows that the information given to a model can matter as much as the model itself.

The evidence on which methods work best for restaurants is mixed. Schmidt, Kabir and Hoque (2022) compared many models on sales data from a single restaurant and found that linear models gave the best forecasts one day ahead, while recurrent neural networks did better one week ahead. So deep learning is not always better for restaurant data, and results depend on the forecast horizon and how the data are prepared.

Retail forecasting research adds a point about forecast level. Fildes, Ma and Kolassa (2022) separate total forecasts for strategic decisions from product level forecasts for day to day operational decisions, and name promotions as a main challenge at product level. For a restaurant, the operational decision is how many portions of each dish to prepare, so the natural forecast level is the menu item on a given day, not total portions sold across the business. A restaurant forecasting system should be judged at the level of the decision it supports, use known drivers of demand such as promotions, calendar effects and weather, and be tested against simple options, not assumed better for being more complex.

## 2.4 Statistical, machine learning and deep learning forecasting

Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021) present the main families of statistical forecasting, including exponential smoothing and ARIMA models, and stress that every forecast should be compared with simple benchmarks such as the mean of past values, the most recent value, or the value from the same season last cycle. A method that cannot beat them gives no useful information, however advanced.

Large comparisons on real data have tested whether machine learning beats statistical methods. Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2018) compared popular machine learning methods with eight traditional statistical methods on 1,045 monthly series from the M3 competition, finding the machine learning methods less accurate for all horizons and measures and more costly to compute. The M5 competition, built on daily unit sales of Walmart products with prices, promotions and calendar information, gave a different picture. Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2022) report that the best entries, based on machine learning, beat the statistical benchmarks, with gradient boosted tree models such as LightGBM common among the top entries. So statistical methods held their place on many short, separate series with little extra information, while on large groups of related series with explanatory variables, machine learning methods learning from many series at once came out ahead.

The tree based methods that did well here have a long history. Breiman (2001) introduced random forests, which average many trees grown on resampled data to give stable predictions that capture interactions between variables. Friedman (2001) described gradient boosting, in which trees are added one at a time, each fixing the errors of those before it. Ke et al. (2017) made gradient boosting much faster on large data sets with LightGBM, which groups feature values into histograms. These methods suit tabular data where each row describes one item on one day, with its price, promotion status and calendar features, as many restaurant and retail problems do. So no method is best everywhere. Performance depends on the data structure, the information available and the level of aggregation, and claims that one method is better need a comparison with simple benchmarks under the same conditions.

## 2.5 LSTM, Prophet and the existing comparisons between them

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) proposed the Long Short Term Memory network, which eases the difficulty ordinary recurrent networks have in learning links across long sequences. Its memory cells and gates control what information is kept and passed on, making it a popular choice for time series forecasting. Prophet works differently. Taylor and Letham (2018) designed it as a model for business time series that adds together a trend, seasonal parts and holiday effects, and can also include explanatory variables. It aims to give analysts sensible, clear forecasts quickly. Because its seasonal terms are estimated from data, a yearly pattern is only learned reliably when training history covers more than one year.

Many studies compare these models with each other and with classical methods, and conclusions vary with the data and setup. Siami-Namini, Tavakoli and Siami Namin (2018) reported that an LSTM cut forecast errors greatly compared with ARIMA on economic and financial series. Menculini et al. (2021), forecasting wholesale food prices, found that ARIMA and LSTM performed about the same, a mix of convolutional and LSTM networks was most accurate, and Prophet was quick and easy to use but much less accurate. Schmidt, Kabir and Hoque (2022) found that the best model for restaurant sales changed with the forecast horizon. So neither LSTM nor Prophet is reliably better, and the result depends strongly on what each model is given and how it is set up.

This shows a common weakness. Many comparisons give each model different information, for example a single series for one and a rich set of explanatory variables for another, or careful tuning for one and defaults for another. A difference in error then cannot be linked to the algorithm. A fair comparison keeps the information the same, tests every model on the same unseen period and includes simple benchmarks. Where richer information is also of interest, its effect should be measured separately, for example by running each model with and without the extra inputs.

## 2.6 Evaluating forecasts fairly

How errors are measured affects which model looks best. Hyndman and Koehler (2006) showed that percentage measures such as the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) cannot be calculated when actual values are zero and are unstable when close to zero. Scale dependent measures, such as the mean absolute error (MAE) and the root mean squared error (RMSE), avoid this but cannot be compared across series of different sizes. Kolassa and Schütz (2007) proposed the ratio of MAE to the mean actual value, which equals the weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE). It can still be calculated when single values are zero and weights each period by its size, so WAPE suits demand data where some items sell nothing on some days.

None of these measures is an accuracy percentage. Reporting one hundred minus WAPE as accuracy is tempting but not a standard measure. It has no lower limit at zero and can look high for a model that explains almost none of the day to day change in a large total. It is better to report errors directly, with the coefficient of determination (R²), which compares a model with simply predicting the average every time. An R² close to zero means the model does no better than that, whatever its WAPE.

The test period matters as much as the measure. Tashman (2000) recommended testing forecasts on data not used to fit the model, ideally from several forecast origins so the result does not depend on one period. Rolling origin evaluation moves the start of the test period forward and refits the model each time, giving several error estimates instead of one. Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) showed that the order of observations must be kept when testing time series. The last part of the series is the standard choice for testing.

A test is only valid if the model cannot see information unavailable at forecast time. Kaufman et al. (2012) call this leakage, where information about the target gets into the inputs or training data, giving results that look excellent in testing but cannot be repeated in real use. Leakage can be hard to spot and can come from history features that include the day being predicted, or from totals that depend on what really happened in the test period. So every input must be checked as truly known before forecasting.

Last, differences between models need significance tests. Diebold and Mariano (1995) proposed a test of whether two forecasts have the same expected loss, based on the differences between their errors. Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold (1997) showed that the original test rejects too often in small samples and proposed a correction using the t distribution. With a test period of a few months, this correction matters. The test gives a sound way to decide whether a model that seems better really is.

## 2.7 From forecasts to preparation decisions

A forecast is only useful when it helps a decision. Food prepared for a service but not sold is usually wasted, and food not prepared means lost sales. This is the setting of the newsvendor problem, in which an amount of a perishable product is chosen before uncertain demand is known, weighing the cost of too much against the cost of too little. Petruzzi and Dada (1999) review the newsvendor model and its extensions. Silver, Pyke and Thomas (2016) show how stock decisions are set by balancing these costs and choosing a target service level.

This matters for preparation planning in two ways. First, a forecast of expected demand differs from the right amount to prepare. Kitchens prepare a safety margin above expected sales, and its size sets the balance between waste and unmet demand. A better forecast changes this balance, so the same service level can be reached with a smaller margin, or a higher level with the same margin. Second, it suggests how to test a planning method when waste has not been measured. A preparation rule can be replayed on past days, comparing what it would have prepared with what was sold. Such a simulation does not measure waste and rests on assumptions, for example that sales equal demand and that unsold portions are thrown away, but it compares methods on the same days in a clear, repeatable way. Its uncertainty can be estimated by resampling the test days with the bootstrap of Efron and Tibshirani (1993).

## 2.8 Anomaly detection in operational data

Unusual days matter in demand planning because they can distort models, point to events needing attention, or reveal data errors. Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar (2009) separate point anomalies, which are single observations that differ from the rest, from contextual and collective anomalies. For daily sales totals, the simplest methods treat each day as a point and ask whether it is far from the usual range.

Two classical rules are widely used. The Z score shows how many standard deviations an observation is from the mean, and a common rule flags values whose Z score is more than three. The mean and standard deviation are themselves affected by extreme values, so a quartile based rule is often used alongside. Tukey (1977) proposed fences based on the interquartile range (IQR), the distance between the first quartile (Q1) and the third quartile (Q3). An observation is flagged if below Q1 minus 1.5 times the IQR or above Q3 plus 1.5 times the IQR. Set from the quartiles, not the mean, the fences are robust to the extreme values they are meant to find.

## 2.9 Decision support systems

A forecasting model does not help a restaurant unless its outputs reach decision makers in a form they can trust. Arnott and Pervan (2014) noted the growing role in decision support research of design science, in which researchers build and test artefacts meant to support real decisions, and raised concerns that much academic work is not relevant to practice. Hevner et al. (2004) set out guidelines for design science requiring that the artefact deals with a relevant problem, that it is tested with rigour and that the research adds clearly explained knowledge. So a forecasting tool should be dependable to install and run, honest about uncertainty and about what has and has not been measured, show results at the level of the decision, and be tested against its requirements, with evidence of what was seen.

## 2.10 Summary and research gap

The research shows that food service is a large source of food waste (WRAP, 2013, United Nations Environment Programme, 2024), that much of it can be avoided (Betz et al., 2015), and that stopping extra food from being made sits at the top of the food waste hierarchy (Papargyropoulou et al., 2014). Restaurant demand is driven by promotions, calendar effects and other known factors (Tanizaki et al., 2019, Fildes, Ma and Kolassa, 2022). No forecasting method is best in all settings (Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos, 2018, 2022). Comparisons between LSTM, Prophet and other methods are many but often reach different conclusions, because the models get different information and settings (Menculini et al., 2021, Schmidt, Kabir and Hoque, 2022).

Few studies of restaurant demand seem to combine, in one work, a comparison that keeps the information given to each model the same and tests differences for significance, a test at the level of the preparation decision, a way to turn forecast quality into effects on overproduction and unmet demand, and a working tool that shows these results honestly. This project addresses them through four research questions. RQ1 asks whether LSTM, Prophet and a Random Forest differ in forecasting daily total demand when given the same information and are tested on the same unseen days. This responds to published comparisons often confusing the algorithm with its inputs. RQ2 asks whether a feature based model that predicts demand for each menu item at each restaurant on each day beats the historical average rule a kitchen might use instead. This follows the argument that forecasts should be tested at the level of the decision. RQ3 asks how much overproduction a preparation plan based on such a model would avoid compared with that rule, answered by simulation with stated assumptions because the data contain no waste measurements. RQ4 asks whether these methods can be delivered as a reliable decision support application that reports its results honestly and is checked against its requirements. This reflects the design science view that an artefact must be usable and tested with rigour.
